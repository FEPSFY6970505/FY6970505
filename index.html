Abstract 

      As mobile devices become more powerful, battery life, charging speed and safety are becoming increasingly important. 
      These are some of the main factor’s consumers consider when purchasing any new device, but in modern day devices – especially mobile devices – still use lithium-ion batteries, which faces limitations like degradation. 
      This article therefore explores how solid-state and semi-solid-state batteries could improve energy storage, there benefits for everyday users, whilst 
      considering the issues with affordability and reliability compared to modern -day batteries.

Introduction

      From attending online lectures to working on a laptop while travelling, people depend on mobile devices throughout the day.
      Yet increased usage on demanding apps and processing power can drain batteries quickly. Manufacturers therefore need to store more energy without making devices less portable or heavier.
      Conventional lithium-ion batteries move lithium ions between electrodes through a liquid electrolyte. 
      So, as an alternative, the solid-state batteries replace this liquid with a solid material, while semi-solid-state batteries retain some gel-like components.  
      However, both technologies are still being developed or enhanced and neither guarantee better performance in every situation.

Analysis and Discussion

2.1 Moving towards Solid-state Technology Lithium-ion batteries are widely used because their manufacturing processes are established and relatively economical. Fully solid-state batteries can require new materials, precise manufacturing and reliable contact between their components. This makes large-scale production extremely difficult (Janek and Zeier, 2023).
But semi- solid designs could offer a more reliable performance. By keeping some liquid or gel-like substance, they can avoid difficulties associated with completely solid electrolytes- like gaps between the materials inside the battery. However, semi-solid and fully solid-state batteries shouldn’t be treated as ‘identical’ technologies, since their designs and performance can vary.
2.2 Safety and Thermal Runaway Safety is a major contribution to why researchers are interested in solid-state batteries. In conventional lithium-ion cells, damage or overheating or overcharging can lead to thermal runaway; a chain reaction which ultimately means a battery produces heat faster than it is escaping. So, a flammable liquid electrolyte can make a fire risk even more severe.
Replacing the liquids with suitable solid-state electrolytes can significantly reduce some fire risks (Faraday Institution, 2020). However, solid-state batteries are not automatically fireproof. Lithium dendrites, tiny, needle-like metal deposits, can still form and cause short circuit. Researchers have demonstrated that this remains as an important problem (Liu et al., 2025).
2.3 Energy Density and Everyday Performance Energy density means the amount of energy stored for a given battery weight or volume. Higher energy density matter especially in portable devices, where space is limited. Some solid-state designs could use lithium- metal anodes, which could potentially store more energy than conventional graphite-based cells (Faraday Institution, 2020).

For example, imagine two laptops with batteries of the same size and weight, but the one storing more usable energy could last longer between charges, without becoming heavier. Alternatively, a manufacturer could make a smartphone thinner while keeping a similar battery life. This would be useful for everyday students or professionals who use their devices throughout the day without always having to access a charger.
But these are the possibilities, not promises: actual battery life depends on the screen, processor and the main software. Solid-state technology might also support faster charging, but charging speed depends on ion movement, resistance, temperature and the design of the charging system. It cannot be assumed that every solid-sate battery will charge faster (Janek and Zeier, 2023).
2.4 Lifespan and Environmental Impact Repeated charging gradually reduces battery capacity. Some solid-state designs may slow degradation processes, potentially allowing devices to remain useful for longer. For students or people who rely on laptops daily, this could mean fewer battery replacements and lower costs over time.
However, a longer lifespan isn’t guaranteed. Environmental impacts can depend on the energy and materials used in manufacturing, as well as whether batteries can be repaired or recycled sustainably – which can lead to potential E-waste through multiple trials.
2.5 Engineering and the Challenges An important engineering challenge is maintaining contact between the electrodes and solid electrolyte. Battery materials expand and contract in different conditions and during use, which can cause internal issues likes gaps, resistance and reduced performance (Janek and Zeier, 2023).
Manufacturers also need to demonstrate consistent safety, durability and reasonable costs across millions of cells throughout the trial and pitching process. Battery management systems will still be needed to monitor voltage, temperature and charging. Therefore, even if research is advancing, successful laboratory results do not automatically mean a new piece of technology is ready to be utilised within mobile devices or laptops.

Conclusion

Solid state and semi-solid-state batteries could improve future mobile devices through safer designs and potentially longer lifespans. For users, this might mean lighter laptops, longer-lasting smartphones and less frequent charging.
However, technical difficulties like production costs, E-waste from trials and uncertain long-term durability remain as significant obstacles. Semi-solid batteries may help as a supporting bridge between these lithium-ion batteries whilst solid-state designs are still being developed. Ultimately, their success depends on providing reliable improvements at a price consumers and manufactures find affordable.
