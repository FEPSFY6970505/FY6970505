The Future of Power Supplies for Mobile Devices: Solid-State and Semi-Solid-State Batteries

Table of Contents
  • Abstract

  • Conclusion

Abstract

As mobile devices become more powerful, battery life, charging speed and safety are increasingly important. These are key factors consumers consider when buying a device. However, most mobile devices still rely on lithium-ion batteries, which face limitations such as degradation. This article explores how solid-state and semi-solid-state batteries could improve energy storage and benefit everyday users, while considering their affordability and reliability.

Introduction

From attending online lectures to working on a laptop while travelling, people depend on mobile devices throughout the day. Yet demanding apps and increased processing power can drain batteries quickly. Manufacturers therefore need to store more energy without making devices heavier.

Conventional lithium-ion batteries move lithium ions between electrodes through a liquid electrolyte. Solid-state batteries replace this liquid with a solid material, while semi-solid batteries retain some liquid or gel-like components. Both alternatives are still developing, and neither guarantees better performance in every situation.

Analysis and Discussion

2.1 Moving Towards Solid-State Technology

Lithium-ion batteries are widely used because their manufacturing processes are established and relatively economical. Fully solid-state batteries may require new materials, precise manufacturing and reliable contact between components. This makes large-scale production difficult (Janek and Zeier, 2023) .

Semi-solid designs could offer a more practical transition. Their liquid or gel-like components can help fill tiny gaps between battery materials, improving contact. However, semi-solid and fully solid-state batteries differ in design and performance. Industry assessments also highlight the challenges of scaling new battery technologies (International Energy Agency, 2026) .

2.2 Safety and Thermal Runaway

Safety is a major reason researchers are interested in solid-state batteries. In conventional lithium-ion cells, damage, overheating or overcharging can trigger thermal runaway: a chain reaction where the battery generates heat faster than it escapes. Flammable liquid electrolytes can make resulting fires more severe.

Replacing these liquids with suitable solid electrolytes could reduce some fire risks (Faraday Institution, 2020) . However, solid-state batteries are not automatically fireproof. Lithium dendrites, tiny needle-like metal deposits, can still form and cause short circuits (Liu et al., 2025) .

2.3 Energy Density and Everyday Performance

Energy density means how much energy a battery stores for its weight or volume. This matters in portable devices where space is limited. Some solid-state designs could use lithium-metal anodes, potentially storing more energy than conventional graphite-based cells (Faraday Institution, 2020) .

For example, two laptops could have batteries of the same size and weight, but the one storing more usable energy could last longer between charges. Similarly, manufacturers could make smartphones thinner without sacrificing battery life. This could benefit students and professionals who use their devices throughout the day.

These are possibilities, not promises: actual battery life also depends on the screen, processor and software. Solid-state technology might support faster charging, but charging speed depends on ion movement, resistance, temperature and charger design (Janek and Zeier, 2023) .

2.4 Lifespan and Environmental Impact

Repeated charging gradually reduces battery capacity. Some solid-state designs may slow certain degradation processes, potentially allowing devices to remain useful for longer. This could mean fewer replacements and lower costs for people who rely on laptops daily.

However, longer lifespans are not guaranteed. Environmental impacts also depend on manufacturing energy, raw materials and whether batteries can be repaired or recycled. Newer technology is not automatically more sustainable.

2.5 Engineering Challenges

An important challenge is maintaining contact between electrodes and solid electrolytes. Battery materials expand and contract during use, which can create tiny gaps, increase resistance and reduce performance (Janek and Zeier, 2023) .

Manufacturers must demonstrate consistent safety, durability and affordable production. Battery management systems will still monitor voltage, temperature and charging. Successful laboratory results do not automatically mean a battery is ready for everyday mobile devices.

Conclusion

Solid-state and semi-solid-state batteries could improve future mobile devices through safer designs, higher energy storage and potentially longer lifespans. For users, this might mean lighter laptops, longer-lasting smartphones and less frequent charging.

However, manufacturing costs and uncertain durability remain significant obstacles. Semi-solid batteries may offer a bridge while fully solid-state designs develop. Ultimately, success depends on delivering reliable improvements at an affordable price.
